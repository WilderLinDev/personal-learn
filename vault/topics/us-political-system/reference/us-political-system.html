US Political System

One country, three branches, three levels of government, and competing political parties.

Congress

House

435 voting members

Seats based on state population; two-year terms.

Senate

100 senators

Two per state; six-year terms.

Why two chambers?

The House represents people by population. The Senate gives every state equal representation.

Rule

Valid federal law can override conflicting state law. But federal power must come from the Constitution. The Tenth Amendment reserves other powers to states or the people.

Political parties

  • Democrats and Republicans are the two dominant national parties; smaller parties and independents also exist.
  • Parties recruit candidates, organise voters, raise funds and coordinate policy.
  • Parties are not constitutional branches. The President, Congress and courts keep their separate legal powers.
  • Party platforms are snapshots of priorities at a particular time; voters and politicians inside each party are not identical.

Public opinion snapshot — 2026

  • Pew, April 2026: 58% viewed the Republican Party unfavourably; 59% viewed the Democratic Party unfavourably; 26% viewed both unfavourably.
  • Gallup, September 2026: trust in the executive 33%, legislative 28%, judicial 46%, state government 55%, local government 67%.
  • These are dated survey results, not a description of every individual.

Five questions for any political news story

  1. Federal, state or local?
  2. Which branch?
  3. What constitutional or legal authority?
  4. Which party or coalition is involved? Are there internal disagreements?
  5. Is this a law, a proposal, a court ruling, a poll or an opinion?
