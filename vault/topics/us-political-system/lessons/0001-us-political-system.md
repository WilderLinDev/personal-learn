:::key[The core model]
Think in three dimensions.

1. **Branches:** Who makes, carries out and interprets the law?
2. **Levels:** Is the decision federal, state or local?
3. **Parties:** Which political coalition is trying to win or use power?

Political parties are not a fourth branch. They compete inside the constitutional system.
:::

## 1. Separation of powers: three branches

The Constitution divides the federal government into three branches. This is the **separation of powers（权力分立）**. The goal is to stop one person or institution from controlling the whole government.

::::cards
:::card[Article I]{foot="Can investigate and impeach officials"}
### Legislative（立法）

**Congress（国会）**

Makes federal laws, approves spending and oversees the executive branch.
:::

:::card[Article II]{foot="Can veto bills and nominate judges"}
### Executive（行政）

**President（总统）**, Vice President, Cabinet and agencies

Enforces federal laws and manages the federal administration.
:::

:::card[Article III]{foot="Can rule a law unconstitutional in a case"}
### Judicial（司法）

**Federal courts（联邦法院）**

Interpret laws and decide whether government action is allowed by the Constitution.
:::
::::

The system of **checks and balances（制衡机制）** connects the branches:

| Action | Check |
|---|---|
| Congress passes a bill. | The President may sign it or veto it. Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in both chambers. |
| The President nominates many senior officials and federal judges. | The Senate confirms or rejects many of these nominations. |
| The government applies a law. | A court may decide, in a real case, that the law or action violates the Constitution. |
| The President or another federal official abuses power. | The House may impeach; the Senate holds the trial and may convict and remove the official. |

:::note
A court does not review every law automatically. A legal dispute must normally reach the courts through a case.
:::

### Congress has two chambers

Congress is **bicameral（两院制）**: it has a House of Representatives and a Senate.

| House of Representatives（众议院） | Senate（参议院） |
|---|---|
| 435 voting members | 100 members |
| Seats are divided by state population | Every state has two senators |
| Two-year terms | Six-year terms |
| More directly reflects population changes | Gives small and large states equal representation |

For example, California has far more people than Wyoming. California therefore has more seats in the House, but both states have two senators. The two chambers must normally pass the same text before a bill can go to the President.

## 2. What are the main federal institutions?

### Executive branch: the President and 15 departments

The President leads the executive branch. The Cabinet is made up mainly of the heads of the 15 executive departments. The departments run major parts of the federal administration.

| Department | Main work |
|---|---|
| State | Foreign relations and diplomacy |
| Treasury | Federal revenue, public debt and financial policy |
| Defense | Armed forces and national defence |
| Justice | Federal law enforcement and legal work; includes the FBI |
| Interior | Federal public lands, national parks and natural resources |
| Agriculture | Farming, food and rural programmes |
| Commerce | Business, trade, economic data and technology policy |
| Labor | Workplace rules and labour statistics |
| Health and Human Services | Public health and major health programmes |
| Housing and Urban Development | Housing and urban development programmes |
| Transportation | Federal transport policy and infrastructure programmes |
| Energy | Energy policy and nuclear research |
| Education | Federal education programmes and civil-rights enforcement |
| Veterans Affairs | Health care and benefits for veterans |
| Homeland Security | Border security, immigration enforcement, disaster response and transport security |

There are also agencies and commissions outside the 15 departments. Examples include the CIA, Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), NASA, Social Security Administration (SSA), Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and Federal Reserve. Their legal structures are not all the same: some are closely directed by the President, while some have statutory independence.

### Legislative and judicial institutions

- **Congress:** the House and Senate, supported by offices such as the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) and Government Accountability Office (GAO).
- **Federal courts:** federal district courts, courts of appeals and the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court is the highest federal court.

Do not confuse federal courts with state courts. Every state also has its own court system.

## 3. How do the 50 states relate to one another and to Washington?

This is **federalism（联邦制）**: constitutional power is divided between the national government and state governments. The states are not ordinary departments managed by the President. Each state has its own constitution, governor, legislature, courts, laws and budget.

| Federal government | State governments | Shared or overlapping areas |
|---|---|---|
| National defence, foreign relations, currency, immigration rules, federal taxes and interstate commerce | Most public-school operations, state police, many criminal laws, family law, land use, professional licences and driver licences | Health care, transport, environmental rules, disaster response, elections and public safety |

The exact boundary depends on the Constitution, federal statutes and court decisions. Many policy areas involve both levels.

:::key[Important limit]
The Supremacy Clause（最高法律条款） makes valid federal law higher than conflicting state law. But this does not mean the federal government can regulate anything it wants. It must act within powers granted by the Constitution. The Tenth Amendment（第十修正案） reserves powers not given to the federal government, and not forbidden to states, to the states or the people.
:::

### States are members of one union, not 50 separate countries

- A state cannot make its own foreign policy, issue a separate national currency or leave the Union by simply passing a state law.
- States can make different laws within their constitutional powers. This is why rules on schools, taxes, policing and many other topics can differ by state.
- Under Article IV, states must give legal respect to many public acts, records and court judgments from other states.
- In the Senate, every state has two senators. In the House, representation depends on population. This is the constitutional compromise between state equality and population equality.

Local governments, such as counties and cities, usually get their powers from state law. They are important, but they are not a third sovereign level equal to the federal and state governments.

## 4. What is the relationship between the two major parties?

The two dominant national parties are the **Democratic Party** and the **Republican Party**. They are political organisations, not branches of government. They recruit candidates, raise money, publish platforms and organise voters at federal, state and local levels. Smaller parties and independent candidates also exist.

| Democratic Party | Republican Party |
|---|---|
| Its 2024 platform placed emphasis on health-care costs, child care, social programmes, clean energy, climate action and gun safety. | Its 2024 platform placed emphasis on border enforcement, tax cuts, deregulation, energy production, defence and gun rights. |

:::note
These are examples from the parties' 2024 platforms, not a complete description of every voter or elected official. Both parties contain different factions, and their positions can change over time.
:::

Parties compete for the presidency, Congress, governorships, state legislatures and local offices. Sometimes one party controls the presidency while the other controls one or both chambers of Congress. This is called **divided government（分立政府）**. It can make lawmaking harder because institutions and parties must negotiate.

Even when one party controls all three federal branches, the branches do not become one institution. Their legal powers remain separate. Judges, members of Congress and the President have different roles and incentives.

## 5. How do Americans feel about the system?

There is no single American opinion. Some people strongly identify with a party. Others dislike both parties, feel tired of politics or hold mixed views on different issues.

### Party feelings: both parties receive negative views

A Pew Research Center survey of 5,103 US adults, conducted April 20–26, 2026, found:

:::stats
- **58%** had an unfavourable view of the Republican Party
- **59%** had an unfavourable view of the Democratic Party
- **26%** had an unfavourable view of both parties
:::

At the same time, most people who identify strongly with a party view their own party positively and the other party negatively. This is one reason political debate can feel very personal.

### But America is not simply divided into two equal groups

Pew's 2026 political typology identified nine groups based on people's values and attitudes, not just party labels. The study found that most Americans were in groups with more mixed political views. A person may be conservative on taxes, moderate on immigration and liberal on another issue. A party label does not tell you everything about a person.

### Trust in government: local government scores higher

In Gallup's September 2026 survey, 33% of US adults trusted the executive branch, 28% trusted the legislative branch and 46% trusted the judicial branch a great deal or a fair amount. Trust was higher for state government (55%) and local government (67%). These are survey results at one point in time, not permanent facts about every American.

:::key[Ideal versus reality]
Many Americans learn that checks and balances protect liberty and prevent concentrated power. In daily politics, however, the same system can look slow, divided and frustrating. Some people see this as a useful limit on power; others see it as gridlock. Both reactions exist.
:::

## 6. A simple method for reading US political news

:::steps
1. **Which level?** Federal, state or local?
2. **Which branch?** Congress, President/agencies or courts?
3. **Which legal power?** Does the Constitution or a law give this institution authority?
4. **Which political actors?** Which party controls the office, and are there disagreements inside that party?
5. **What is the evidence?** Is the story reporting a law, a court ruling, a proposal, a poll or someone's opinion?
:::
